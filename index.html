Why onchain is inevitable

The ledger is settled. The fight is whether it’s open.

In January someone asked me, again, why crypto would ever “come back”. I answered with a list. Have you executed a trade through Etherscan because the frontend was down. Bought an asset on one venue and sold it on another. Read a stranger’s wallet to see how they made their money. Sent money and watched it arrive. Borrowed at 3am against rules you could read, from nobody. How do you even go back. Predicting the future from the current state of an industry is what a tourist does.

That was a post. This is the argument, second draft. The first claimed that most of the world’s money ends up onchain, and on rereading, every objection it answered was one I expected to beat. So this one claims less and fights harder. The claim: every ledger is becoming programmable, and that is no longer contested. The fight that matters is whether the base those ledgers settle on is open. Open wins that fight where it matters, and loses it in places I will name. Three steps, one adversary, and a map.

A superset of rights

A dollar is a bundle of rights: to hold it, move it, pledge it, lend it, exchange it; with whom, at what hour, on whose permission. Forms of money differ in which rights they carry, and the rights of bank money are fixed in its base layer. The ledger closes on weekends. Only licensed institutions touch it. Every transfer passes an approval. Counterparties stop at the border. Onchain money’s base has one rule: a valid signature moves value between keys, to anyone, at any hour. Everything else is optional.

The relationship between the two is not “different”. It runs one way. Take open money and you can add every restriction a bank has, as a layer. Custody, and you have a bank account. An issuer freeze list, and a court order works. A whitelist, and only approved wallets can hold it. Escrow and timelocks, and payments reverse. Each is a permissioned product running on a permissionless base. Now go the other way. Build “pay a stranger in another country at 3am, final in seconds, no approval” on top of ACH. You cannot; the constraint lives below you. The apps that feel instant, Venmo, Cash App, get there by keeping both parties inside one company’s ledger, and the moment you leave the garden you are back on the base’s clock. Venmo is instant Venmo-to-Venmo. A stablecoin is instant to anyone.

this is the whole argument now. a superset of rights, and costs that fall on one side only.

So everything buildable on the closed base is buildable on the open one, plus what the closed one cannot host at all: lending with no lender’s permission, payment between two machines with no legal person on either end. More rights are worth more. Optionality has a price, and where the restriction binds you can read it off the market: in every capital-controlled economy the dollar trades above the official rate in stablecoin form. Argentina, Nigeria, Turkey, anyone who has needed a Chinese OTC desk. Capital moving toward a premium is not a preference for freedom. It is arbitrage, and it started where the premium was highest. That wave already happened. Nobody voted on it.

The premium gives the direction. The speed is set by cost.

Costs fall on one side only

Four costs stand between a dollar and the open base: the risk of losing it, the friction of using it, the law around it, and the privacy it gives up. The first three fall with scale and time. Theft as a share of value secured has fallen for years. Passkeys and account abstraction are turning self-custody from a chore into a default. The law is forming by statute and rulemaking instead of by lawsuit. On the other side nothing falls, because the restrictions on bank money are not costs waiting for an engineer. A bank that let anyone pay anyone with no approval would lose its licence. The restriction is the product, the state’s control surface, and it will still be there in 2040. Falling cost against a fixed restriction is a one-way convergence.

The fourth cost is the honest exception. A bank balance is invisible to your neighbour; an onchain balance is not. No company runs payroll where competitors can read headcount, and no fund trades where its book is public before settlement. Privacy is the gate on institutional money, and the fix is both unfinished, zero-knowledge proofs with selective disclosure, and contested, because the regulator who wants transparency is the one the institutions answer to. The internet had the same hole: IP lacked the quality guarantees of a telephone circuit, and in 1998 that was the whole case for why it would never carry voice. It carried voice because bandwidth got cheap faster than the missing feature mattered. I expect the same of privacy, on a horizon of a decade rather than a year, and if the clock in this essay is wrong, it is wrong here.

the one cost I can’t wave away. if the clock is wrong, it’s wrong here.

The objection that usually arrives at this point is theft, now with AI finding the bugs. It does find them: Anthropic’s agents reproduced half of 405 historical DeFi exploits in simulation, and pointed at 2,849 fresh contracts found two zero-days worth $3,694 at an API cost of $3,476. The money has not followed the headlines: 4 percent of incidents produce 75 percent of losses, and those are stolen keys and deceived humans, not code, and contracts are the only part of finance that can be formally proven. The attack that matters is on people, and there bank money is the exposed side. Its security is identity, prove you are you, and AI attacks identity directly: a bank chairman wired €95 million last month on the strength of a WhatsApp and a cloned voice. Onchain security is a key, and a deepfake cannot sign. The honest cost is that reversibility needs an adjudicator, an adjudicator needs identity, so identity comes back in at the issuer: the surface moves from every branch to a few compliance desks. Smaller, not zero.

Two accelerants

Two things raise the rate, and neither depends on anyone preferring anything.

A market can only express what it can list. Traditional finance lists what a committee approves, on a quarter’s notice. The open base lists anything, in minutes, into one global pool, so the set of states money can be in grows on one side only: an outcome, an attention cycle, an agent’s revenue, a cohort of robots. Most of what gets listed is worthless, which is compatible with a claim about the rail and never the asset. By construction, the open base’s lead in next moves widens every year.

And an AI agent cannot open a bank account. It has no legal personhood and no business hours; every bank rail assumes a person at the end and a human clock; a chain assumes a key. Whatever share of transactions agents come to do is onchain by necessity. The card networks know it, which is why their agent-payment products settle in stablecoins. It is the one force in this argument that does not route through a human at all.

That is the case for. Now the opponent that can win.

The real adversary

The adversary is not the bank. Banks have conceded the architecture; what they contest is who runs the base. JPMorgan clears about $2 billion a day on a private ledger. Partior and Fnality settle between banks the same way. DTCC can build its own; China built e-CNY. If the institutions that hold most of the world’s money tokenize on ledgers they control, the architecture wins and the openness loses, and “blockchain, not Bitcoin”, mocked in 2016, is the ending. This is the strongest objection to the essay, and the first draft never named it.

The answer is that an island needs a sea. A permissioned ledger holding only its owner’s clients is a database with extra steps. Its value is in reaching counterparties the owner does not control, and the only neutral place to meet a counterparty you do not control is a base nobody controls. After five years on its private ledger, JPMorgan put a deposit token on a public chain. BlackRock put a fund on Ethereum. Nasdaq has asked to trade tokenized stock, and in September the SEC granted a five-year exemption for tokenized stock trading on public, permissionless ledgers, in those words, for permissioned venues built on top of them. The intranet did not lose to the internet. It ran the internet’s protocols, hid behind a firewall, and plugged in. Permissioned ledgers become subnets of the open base, and settlement between them happens on it. A great deal of value stays behind the firewalls, which is why this essay now claims the base and not the balances.

The map

Open loses in three places.

Where the state matches the friction. UPI clears around twenty billion transactions a month. Pix reached most of Brazil in four years. FedNow and instant SEPA exist. None is a blockchain, and together they prove that hours, fees and T+2 were legacy, not structure, and that a state will remove them exactly when crypto threatens, while keeping identity and jurisdiction. India did it, added a 30 percent tax, and domestic exchange volume collapsed. In a country with good rails nobody goes onchain to buy coffee, and the second wave the first draft promised, unrestricted money arriving for convenience, does not arrive. What survives is access. UPI moves rupees between Indians; it does not let an Indian hold a US treasury, or a Brazilian trade at 3am, or anyone hold what their regulator has not listed. A state can match friction inside its border and its unit. It cannot match access across them without giving up the control it kept. UPI killed the payments case for crypto in India and left the savings case untouched, because what people with savings want to hold is not rupees.

hardest objection I got. India is the live test.

Where the state is strong and hostile. The open internet lost in China, and 1.4 billion people live behind that result. Money is more sovereign than information, so anyone arguing from the internet has to concede a map, not a tide: open money wins where the state is weak, indifferent, or the sponsor. Most of the world’s people live in those places. Most of the world’s money sits in the US, the EU, China and Japan, three of which have every reason to keep control. Note what China did, though. It did not go back to Minitel. It built its own internet on the same protocols and put the control at the border. Its money is going the same way: e-CNY is a ledger, the firewall is at the on-ramps, and borders leak, which is why mainland OTC desks move a volume of stablecoins that appears in no official statistic, and why Hong Kong wrote a stablecoin licensing regime at arm’s length.

The dollar. The sovereign argument rests on the reserve issuer sponsoring stablecoins, and it holds: a dollar on an open rail turns every other country’s capital control into demand for it. But it makes the rail a US instrument, and that makes enemies. MiCA caps non-euro stablecoins; India’s hostility is not an accident; the internet was American too, and that produced the splinternet. The first draft also claimed that every network grows a unit of account to match its topology. Its own data falsifies that: the global permissionless network did not grow a unit, it imported the dollar. So the claim shrinks again and gets stronger for it. The open base does not have to win the unit-of-account war. It has to be the arena. The EU’s rational response to a dollar on the rail is not a wall but a euro on the rail, which is what MiCA licenses, and every sovereign’s rational response is the same. The rail wins because everyone’s unit ends up on it, not because any one unit does.

struck the “every network grows its own unit” line. the internet imported the dollar.

Information went first

The reason to believe open wins the fight it can win is that the fight has been run before, under the same word. In the nineties they called it the Crypto Wars and meant cryptography: the Clipper chip, encryption on the Munitions List, Zimmermann investigated for three years for publishing PGP, a federal court needed to say that code is speech. The network it was fought over was built on a principle written down in 1984: do as little as possible in the network and leave every function to the endpoints, because a network that builds a function in imposes it on everyone and forbids what it did not anticipate. IP’s rivals, CompuServe, AOL, Minitel, the telephone companies, were better at everything they chose to do, and lost because an open base can host a closed service and a closed base cannot host an open one. Nobody could have built the Web inside CompuServe. Berners-Lee built it on IP without asking anyone. Twenty years of services followed on the base that did not need permission, and the smart networks became apps on the dumb one.

Two corrections to the analogy, both against me. Information was never a core function of the state and money is, which is why this is slower and why the map exists. And internet money’s own record is 0 for 1: DigiCash, e-gold, and the 1998 argument that micropayments were inevitable, which was this argument, and lost to advertising for twenty-five years. Crypto is the second attempt, and after fifteen years it has one product that is not about itself, the stablecoin. One product with a $300 billion float is enough to prove the base. It is not enough to prove the product space, and the next five years, the first in which building the rest is not a crime, will say.

0 for 1. I’d forgotten DigiCash.

For the clock: if 2022 was 2000, this is 2005.

What would make me wrong

The first draft’s test was theft per dollar secured. It still holds and still matters, but I expected it to pass, which made it decoration. These can fail.

these can fail. the draft-1 one couldn’t.

Where tokenized value settles. If at the end of this cycle most tokenized assets sit on permissioned ledgers that never settle to a public one, the islands did not need the sea, and the adversary won.

India and Brazil. If holdings, not domestic exchange volume, fall in the countries with the best state rails, the access premium is not real and the second wave is dead everywhere that matters.

A second product. If by 2030 stablecoins are still the only onchain product at scale that is not about crypto, the product space was as small as the sceptics say. Prediction markets and tokenized treasuries for non-US holders are the candidates.

A treasury. If no large company runs payroll or treasury on a public ledger by the early 2030s, privacy was structural, and the institutional wave was a permissioned wave.

A decade of this looking slow would not falsify the thesis. Any of those four would.

The argument

Money is a bundle of rights; the open base carries the larger bundle; the costs of using it fall while the restrictions on the alternative are the alternative’s whole point. That gives the direction. Agents and permissionless issuance give the rate. The state draws the border, which is where open money stops, and leaks. The ledger is settled. The fight is whether it is open, and the one time this fight was run before, open won everywhere it was allowed to, and became the thing the walls were built out of.

still a draft. it always will be. — WK
